The Privacy–Utility Tradeoff in Genomics Is Not a Tradeoff

A pacing constraint on genomic medicine, the structural reason it persists, and a testable experiment to dissolve it.

Rohan Vinaik · May 1, 2026
Genomic Privacy Hyperdimensional Computing Health Infrastructure Biotech Bottlenecks Information Theory

In April 2026, the NIH's All of Us program holds whole-genome sequences for roughly 250,000 of the 350 million Americans it nominally serves. Sequencing now costs $100–200 per genome, down from $3 billion at Human Genome Project scale. The cost is solved. The vision was there. The delivery wasn't.

The reason has nothing to do with sequencing throughput. The reason is that genomes, once sequenced, can't move. 23andMe filed for bankruptcy in March 2025 with roughly 15 million genomes on its servers and an unresolved question about what happens to them. Multi-institutional GWAS routinely takes longer to legally clear than to scientifically run. Rare-disease consortia exist primarily as legal frameworks for data-use agreements, not as scientific instruments. The bottleneck is structural, not technological — and it is now the pacing constraint on genomic medicine at population scale.

The structure is this. Most working scientists, most regulators, and most security architects operate under the assumption that

Security  ∧  Privacy  ⊥  Utility

— that the more accessible a genome is for analysis, the less private it can be, and that hardening privacy degrades the utility of the data downstream. The constraint is treated as a law. Federated learning preserves utility within a node and trades it across nodes. Differential privacy adds noise calibrated to a privacy budget, which destroys rare-variant signal precisely where it matters most for clinical care. Fully homomorphic encryption preserves utility but at compute costs that put population-scale GWAS into the millions of dollars per query. Each technique conserves the underlying conflict. None dissolves it.

I think the conflict is an artifact of representation, not a property of the data. The bottleneck I want to address is that genomic medicine has no scalable substrate, because every existing privacy-preserving technique operates on the raw nucleotide-level genotype as the canonical analytical unit. If you preserve that representation, the privacy–utility tradeoff is real and unavoidable. If you change the representation, the tradeoff dissolves.

Why It's Hard Within Existing Structures

The privacy–utility tradeoff is hard not because the math is hard, but because every layer of the stack has internalized the genotype as the canonical unit of analysis.

Biology. GWAS, polygenic scores, rare-variant burden tests, ancestry inference, pharmacogenomics, somatic-mutation detection — every clinically deployed analytical pipeline takes per-base genotype calls as input. Tooling, training, validation cohorts, and FDA submission templates all assume that representation.

Statistics. Re-identification attacks (Gymrek et al. 2013; Erlich & Narayanan 2014; Homer et al. 2008; Sankararaman et al. 2009) work because allele frequencies at independent loci form near-unique fingerprints. Differential privacy responds by injecting calibrated noise into those frequencies. That operation is mathematically equivalent to destroying signal at low-frequency variants — exactly the variants clinicians most want to keep.

Regulation. HIPAA's Safe Harbor de-identification rule explicitly does not apply to genomic data, because the genome itself is identifying. GDPR Article 9 treats genetic data as a special category requiring explicit consent for each downstream use. Both regulatory frameworks are written for a representation that has the property of being identifying.

If you stay in the genotype-as-analytical-unit frame, the tradeoff is iron. The hardening of any one of {security, privacy, utility} reduces the others. There is no architectural move within this frame that escapes it. That is what makes the bottleneck structural rather than technological — it is conserved across the privacy-preserving techniques we already have because they all leave the underlying representation untouched.

The Hypothesis

The reframe is biochemical before it is informational. DNA is not base-4. It is base-2² — two orthogonal binary properties of the nucleotides (purine vs. pyrimidine ring count; keto vs. amino functional group) that happen to take four combinatorial values when read out as letters. Encoding a genome as four arbitrary symbols destroys that structure. Encoding it as two independent ternary channels (+1, 0, −1 for each property) preserves it, and lets a hyperdimensional projection on top of those channels do something the genotype representation cannot.

I have built a working prototype of this, called GenomeVault. The full architecture is publishable; the relevant features for this essay are three.

1. Geometric fidelity. The encoding preserves the relationships between genomes — cosine similarity, ancestry clustering, polygenic-score correlations, association statistics — at high fidelity. Across a held-out ClinVar benchmark the prototype reaches 99.978% per-position accuracy at 30 MB per genome with sub-10-microsecond query latency on commodity hardware.

2. Computational irreversibility. The encoding is information-theoretically irreversible without knowledge of which reference strands from a public pool of size k were used at encoding time, and the per-encoding random seed used to mix them. The reconstruction search space exceeds 2²⁵⁶ for any k > a few hundred, and is bounded above by approximately 2⁸⁰⁰ ᵏ for genome-scale parameters. This is not encryption — encryption is reversible by design. It is a one-way projection that preserves the geometry analysis needs while destroying the coordinates re-identification requires.

3. An emergent biophysical signal I did not design in. The "errors" in the encoding — positions where quantization disagrees with the underlying call — concentrate at biologically meaningful sites. In prototype data, error sites show 46× DNase-hypersensitivity enrichment with p < 10⁻¹⁵. The math accidentally surfaces regulatory structure because the representation respects the chemistry. This is not a load-bearing claim of the essay. It is a sanity check that the encoding has not destroyed the biology.

Stated flatly: privacy and analytical utility are not endpoints of a line. They are orthogonal axes. Security (preventing unauthorized access), privacy (making accessed data computationally useless), and utility (preserving the geometry analysis needs) can be co-optimized because they operate on different layers of the encoding.

Is that it? Mostly. None of the individual components is novel — Kanerva-style hyperdimensional computing dates to 1988; differential genome encoding is an active subfield; reference pools and randomization are standard cryptographic primitives. What is new is the combination, and specifically the claim that DNA's biochemical structure permits a representation in which the privacy–utility surface stops being a Pareto frontier.

The Sharpest Objection

The strongest objection is that this sounds like security through obscurity. The argument runs: I have added a randomization step (which reference pool, which mixing seed) and called the resulting irreversibility "privacy." But security through obscurity is brittle by definition — once the architecture leaks, the privacy collapses.

The argument is partially correct, and the response has to be precise. Three things distinguish this from security through obscurity.

First, the public reference pool can be made arbitrarily large. With k = 1,000 strands drawn from the 1000 Genomes Project, the per-genome uncertainty over which reference was used at each chunk grows combinatorially in k and the chunk count. Exhaustive search exceeds 2²⁵⁶ well before genome scale. Increasing k has no runtime cost after encoding completes.

Second, the irreversibility claim is not "the architecture is secret." The architecture is publishable and intended to be published. The claim is "even with the architecture, the encoded file, the public reference pool, the encoding code, and unlimited compute, you cannot reconstruct the original genome without the per-encoding random seed." That is information-theoretic, not obscurity-based. The seed is the cryptographic load-bearing element; the architecture around it is open.

Third, there is an explicit pool-rotation procedure. When iterative-query bit leakage drives the information-theoretic privacy budget below 2¹²⁸ bits — a configurable threshold — the reference pool reshuffles. A sufficiently paranoid deployment can rotate after every query, at the cost of re-encoding.

I want to be clear about what fails in this story. If an attacker gains root-level access to the encoding system at the moment of encoding, they can recover the seed and the reference selection, and the privacy of that single genome is broken. The architecture is not magic. It is a structural reduction of the attack surface from "the database" — where one breach yields all genomes — to "the encoding event," where one breach yields one genome and the next encoding starts the attack over. That is a different and much smaller surface to defend.

The Experiment

The testable claim is: an HDC-on-Z₂×Z₂ encoding preserves analytical utility on cross-institutional genomic queries while reducing re-identification risk below differential-privacy baselines, measurably and reproducibly.

The experiment, scoped to a single year of work and roughly $200K in compute and personnel:

Datasets. Three publicly available genomic resources, encoded independently with disjoint reference pools and seeds: 1000 Genomes Phase 3 (n ≈ 3,200), a UK Biobank synthetic-data subset (n ≈ 10,000), and the gnomAD v4 variant catalog. Encoding each independently is the point — the experiment is whether queries can cross institutional boundaries on encoded data alone.

Utility benchmark. Run a standard GWAS pipeline (regenie or PLINK 2.0) on raw genotypes and on HDC-encoded representations, for three quantitative phenotypes from the synthetic UKBB subset (e.g., height, BMI, LDL). Success criterion: ≥95% concordance of top-1,000 genome-wide-significant hits with the raw-data GWAS, with effect-size estimates inside overlapping 95% CIs.

Privacy benchmark. Adversarially attempt re-identification using the membership-inference attacks of Homer et al. (2008) and Sankararaman et al. (2009) against the encoded representations. Compare false-positive rates against differential-privacy baselines at ε ∈ {0.1, 1.0, 10.0}. Success criterion: re-identification AUC ≤ 0.55 (i.e., near random) at all three epsilon-equivalent privacy budgets, and lower than the DP baselines at matched utility.

Failure modes that would themselves be informative. If utility holds but rare variants (MAF < 0.001) are systematically degraded by quantization, the approach scopes to common-variant analyses and rules itself out for rare-disease consortia. If privacy holds but utility degrades non-uniformly across ancestry groups, that surfaces a fairness constraint that must be designed in before deployment. Either negative result is publishable and forecloses a real branch of the design space, which the field needs.

The experiment is not "GenomeVault works." The experiment is "the privacy–utility surface in this representation is qualitatively different from the standard genotype representation, and here is a measurement of its shape."

Why Now

Three things converged in the last two years that make this experiment ripe.

The first is sequencing cost. At $100–200 per genome, the bottleneck on cohort size is no longer dollars per sample. It is institutional willingness to release data, which is downstream of the privacy–utility constraint. The pacing variable has moved.

The second is that the hyperdimensional-computing literature has matured to where the math is no longer exotic. Kanerva-style binary HDC is a standard tool in neuromorphic computing, with hardware acceleration on commodity GPUs and FPGA implementations. The irreversibility argument used to be theoretical; with current hardware it is also empirical.

The third is regulatory. The 23andMe bankruptcy forced the FTC and several state attorneys general to confront, on the record, that the existing legal framework for genomic data has no clean answer to "what happens to consumer genomes when the company holding them dissolves." HIPAA Safe Harbor explicitly does not cover genomes. GDPR Article 9 was written before population-scale genomic medicine existed. There is, for the first time in fifteen years, regulatory appetite for a representational alternative if one can be demonstrated to work.

Generalizability

The same structural move — that analysis needs the geometry of the data, not the specific coordinates — applies anywhere privacy and utility are in tension and the underlying object has compositional structure. Medical records for cross-hospital phenotype queries. Financial transactions for cross-institutional fraud detection. Behavioral data for public-health surveillance. I am not claiming the same encoding works in all of them. I am claiming the same move is available: identify the canonical representation, ask whether analytical utility actually requires it, and find a representation that preserves the geometry without the coordinates. Genomics is the natural first case because DNA's chemistry hands you the orthogonal-binary structure for free. Other domains will need to find their own.

The privacy–utility tradeoff in genomics is not a law. It is the residue of a representation we never questioned. Once you do question it, the tradeoff stops being a tradeoff, and what looked like a fundamental limit on genomic medicine reveals itself as the pacing constraint we still happen to be working under.

It is dissolvable. The experiment to confirm that costs less than a postdoc.
